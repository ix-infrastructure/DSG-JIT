# Correction layers must beat ORB-SLAM3's own thresholds

An upgraded ORB-SLAM3 pipeline should be a thin, mostly gated layer, not a stack of restoration networks. The per-frame path holds always-on degradation monitors and one photometric lookup table shared by both cameras, which costs under a millisecond on a CPU and only a few lines of latency in the Kria's programmable logic. A denoiser and an IMU-gated deblur switch on only when the physics says they can help. A calibration-health monitor re-estimates parameters only during well-excited motion. After SLAM, a fixed-lag robust smoother runs online and a held-out, honestly labelled correction runs offline. The governing fact is that **stereo-inertial ORB-SLAM3 already has a strong do-nothing baseline for every module**: the FAST threshold for low light, frame dropping plus IMU bridging for blur, Huber kernels for extrinsic drift, and loop closure plus full bundle adjustment for trajectory error. Corrections that ignored those baselines have measurably made SLAM worse. Histogram equalization and adaptive gamma lowered ORB-SLAM3's average accuracy on OIVIO. Frame-wise GAN translation pushed stereo ORB-SLAM to 76% relative error at night. Deblurring worsened DSO on EuRoC MH1 and V1_03. For low light, the module to build first, the sensor arithmetic sets the plan. Within the MT9V034's 1x–4x analog gain (up to 2 EV of scene dimming), mid-grey noise stays near 2–3 DN, about an order of magnitude below FAST's threshold of 20. That predicts no effect, which matches the lab's null results for fixed-pattern and IMU noise. Beyond 2 EV the image darkens, and the clean-image contrast a corner needs to fire FAST doubles with every stop: 40, 80, 160, 320 DN. Detection therefore collapses before noise matters, and brightening is mostly a disguised threshold change. Denoising should start to pay only around 4–5 EV, where brightened noise (about 9–16 DN) approaches the threshold. No published study has run a calibrated Poisson-Gaussian low-light ladder on a visual-inertial benchmark with stereo-inertial ORB-SLAM3, or compared brighten-only against brighten-plus-denoise on trajectory error. The experiment designed below would be new: an 8-level ladder, a lowered-FAST-threshold control arm, 10 paired seeds per cell (a 5 mm minimum detectable effect against the 4 mm nondeterminism floor), and validation on real dark data with frozen parameters. Most other modules' headline questions already have answers in the literature.

## One cheap per-frame path feeds three slower correction loops

The architecture follows from one budget fact: at 20 Hz the per-frame budget is 50 ms, and ORB-SLAM3's own tracker consumes about half of it on a desktop CPU. Desktop RGB-D ORB-SLAM takes **about 25 ms per frame, 16 ms of it ORB extraction**. A Jetson CPU takes 100 ms and an ARM CPU 142 ms ([Vemulapati & Chen, arXiv:2207.08405](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08405)). Stereo tracking costs more than that RGB-D figure. On a Kria KR260, ORB extraction accounts for **68% of tracking time**, and an HLS extractor gave a **10x speedup** on that stage. The same authors name bundle adjustment as the next bottleneck ([Arora, TU Dresden StuFoExpo 2024, abstract only](https://flipbook.webspace.tu-dresden.de/stufoexpo24/files/basic-html/page15.html)). Anything that runs every frame therefore has to cost a few milliseconds at most. Everything heavier has to move to a slower clock: the keyframe rate of ORB-SLAM3's LocalMapping thread, a background calibration thread, or the post-run stage. This split is not speculative. MBA-VO runs a 34.4 ms blur-aware tracker every frame and confines its 140 ms deblurring network to keyframes inside the local mapper ([Liu et al., ICCV 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.13684)). Schneider et al. run camera-IMU calibration "in a background process" on selected informative segments only ([arXiv:1901.07242](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.07242)).

The budget arithmetic differs by platform. On a **desktop**, the tracker leaves roughly 25 ms. Photometric correction and the monitors cost under a millisecond. A curve-estimation network should cost well under a millisecond per camera, since EuRoC frames carry about 9x fewer values than the 1200x900x3 benchmark images; that figure is extrapolated, not measured. A per-frame deblurring network does not fit: 2 x 38 ms exceeds the whole remainder. On a **Jetson**, ORB-SLAM3 mono-inertial has been reported at 35.4 ms per frame ([arXiv:2608.17874, unverified snippet](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.17874v1)). That leaves about 15 ms, so only LUT- and curve-class corrections fit. A separate report of 8.4 ms per frame for monocular ORB-SLAM3 on Orin NX ([Kumar et al., arXiv:2605.03678](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.03678)) conflicts with other measurements and should not be used for planning. On the **Kria**, the natural design puts the photometric LUT and the statistics in the PL streaming path immediately before the ORB accelerator. There they add a few image lines of latency rather than a frame. The ARM-side tracking and bundle adjustment then remain the bottleneck. Learned enhancers or deblurrers on Kria would need a CNN accelerator, and no published FPGA implementation of learned enhancement or deblurring integrated with SLAM was found. If preprocessing is pipelined (correct frame k+1 while frame k is tracked), throughput stays at 20 Hz and end-to-end latency grows by one frame. The IMU can propagate the pose across that delay, but the latency must be reported separately from throughput.

Two hardware facts make the live Kria system more capable than any dataset experiment can show. First, the MT9V034 has a **10-bit column-parallel ADC with an optional 12-to-10-bit companding mode "allowing more accurate digitization for darker areas"** ([MT9V034 datasheet](https://cdn.sparkfun.com/assets/b/0/d/6/7/MT9V034.PDF)). A PL correction can act on those 10 bits before truncation to 8. In dark two-view matching, keeping RAW bit depth mattered about as much as denoising: ORB with histogram equalization scored N_τ = 0.267 on RAW against 0.210 on 8-bit ISP output ([Song et al., MID, ICCV 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.03585)). Second, a live system can change the camera's exposure and gain instead of repairing images afterwards. Noise-aware exposure control chooses exposure and gain in **6.6 ms per update** to avoid the "strong salt-and-pepper noise" of high gain ([Shin et al., IROS 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.12646)). In deployment, the best low-light "correction" may be the exposure policy, and a dataset cannot test that.

Five design rules follow from the documented failure cases discussed in the next section. First, compute photometric parameters from a joint left/right histogram and apply them to both cameras. Independent per-image enhancement risks unequal brightness and texture between views; that is an inference, and no SLAM paper measured it. Second, smooth parameters over time with an EMA and hysteresis. Single-image enhancers "suffer serious temporal instability" on video ([Zhang et al., CVPR 2021](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2021/papers/Zhang_Learning_Temporal_Consistency_for_Low_Light_Video_Enhancement_From_Single_CVPR_2021_paper.pdf)), and MFGAN without temporal and stereo consistency losses drove stereo ORB-SLAM to 76.32% mean relative error ([Jung et al., CoRL 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06632)). Third, keep the per-frame path to physically constrained operators (curves, deconvolution with IMU kernels) and confine generative restoration to keyframes. Fourth, when a module synthesizes pixels, use them for detection and association but take measurements from the original image, or inflate their covariance. Fifth, every gated module must reduce to the identity on clean input and be checked against a clean-data regression row. Swapping ORB for SuperPoint plus LightGlue inside ORB-SLAM3 made stereo-inertial EuRoC worse on V102, V201 and V203, for example V201 went from 0.032 to 0.046 m ([SL-SLAM, arXiv:2405.03413](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.03413)).

### Low light: contrast collapse past 4x gain is the first-order failure, not noise

**Realistic conditions.** Robotics "low light" spans about 0–10 lx of scene illuminance. DarkSLAM measured 5–9 lx "Shaded", 3–5 lx "Semi-dark" and 0–2 lx "Dark" ([Savinykh et al., arXiv:2206.02199](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02199)). Photography datasets go much lower: SID's indoor scenes sit at 0.03–0.3 lx with 100–300x exposure ratios ([Chen et al., CVPR 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01934)), and MID's at 0.02–0.3 lx ([MID](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.03585)). The MT9V034 bounds what this rig can absorb. It offers **analog gain 1x–4x in 6.25% steps (0–12 dB)**, digital gain of 0.25–3.75x, 4.8 V/(lux·s) responsivity and more than 55 dB linear dynamic range ([datasheet](https://cdn.sparkfun.com/assets/b/0/d/6/7/MT9V034.PDF)). At 20 Hz, exposure cannot exceed 50 ms, and motion blur caps it well below that. Analog gain can therefore hide about 2 EV of dimming. Beyond that, the image genuinely darkens. EuRoC itself is a poor dark benchmark. The Machine Hall is described as "dusky", and MH_05 "has many consecutive low-light scenes" ([Light-SLAM, arXiv:2407.02382](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.02382)). But EuRoC publishes **no exposure or gain metadata and no photometric calibration** ([TUM VI paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06120); [Bergmann et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.02081)), and it ran auto-exposure independently per camera ([EuRoC page](https://projects.asl.ethz.ch/datasets/euroc-mav/)). Its darkness has to be measured from pixel statistics. An order-of-magnitude camera-equation estimate says a 1 lx scene delivers about 0.01 lx to the sensor at f/2. EuRoC-class sensors are therefore signal-starved below about 1 lx without active illumination. That estimate rests on an unpublished full-scale voltage, so treat it as an argument rather than a measurement.

The mechanism follows from the lab's own source reading of the fork. FAST fires at 20 gray levels, falling back to 7, on unblurred pyramid levels. Descriptors are computed after a 7x7, σ = 2 Gaussian blur. Matching accepts 50 or 100 differing bits out of 256. Realistic sensor noise sits about 8–10x below the detection thresholds. When light falls but gain compensates, brightness holds and noise grows only to about 2–3 DN (derived below), which is still well under 20. When gain saturates, every contrast in the image shrinks by the attenuation factor while the threshold stays fixed. A linear brightness gain is equivalent to dividing the FAST thresholds by the same factor, apart from rounding and clipping. One further non-equivalence is worth testing. ORB-SLAM3 blurs an 8-bit image before computing BRIEF, so in a very dark frame the blurred output has few distinct levels, many test pairs tie, and descriptors lose distinctiveness. A brightness gain applied before the blur reduces those ties. A lowered threshold does not. This is an inference from the source structure, not a measured effect.

**Prior work on degradation.** Feature-based systems fail by losing tracking or never initializing, not by drifting gracefully. ORB-SLAM2's correct-tracking rate fell **from 41% to 0%** going from 5–9 lx to 0–2 lx ([DarkSLAM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02199)). On AirSLAM's "Dark EuRoC" (V2_01 darkened with a noise-free gamma curve over 12 levels), ORB-SLAM3 failed or exceeded 1 m RMSE at levels 10–11. Optical-flow methods were more stable under uniform darkening, and descriptor methods were more stable under onboard or switched lighting ([AirSLAM, arXiv:2408.03520](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03520)). At photon-floor SNR, ORB-SLAM3 and DSO "emit nothing at all" ([Noctif3R, arXiv:2609.21114, Sept 2026 preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21114)). In dark two-view matching, ORB on 8-bit ISP output reached under 5° pose error on only **2.9%** of exposure settings ([MID](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.03585)).

**Prior work on correction.** Brightness correction carries most of the benefit, and the method matters a great deal. In DarkSLAM, mean ORB inlier matches went from 81 raw to 82–101 with gamma, 200 with histogram equalization and 266 with EnlightenGAN. Only the GAN tracked anything at 0–2 lx: 25.1% of the trajectory against 0.6% for equalization ([DarkSLAM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02199)). In Twilight SLAM, ORB-SLAM3's RMSE on ETH3D's dark sfm_lab_room2 fell from 1.13 m to 0.52–0.56 m with any of four enhancers. That result is monocular and reports the **best of five runs** ([arXiv:2304.11310](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11310)). In MID, global equalization beat a state-of-the-art CNN enhancer for ORB (N_τ 0.210 against 0.125). BM3D denoising added about 11% relative on top of equalization (0.296 against 0.267), against a roughly ninefold gain from any contrast correction at all ([MID](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.03585)). FFDNet denoising lowered ORB-SLAM2's ATE by 16.75% even on clean sequences, and tracking failed only above σ ≈ 12.75 DN of synthetic Gaussian noise ([Cao et al., Sensors 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/s20174922)). Adaptive FAST thresholds are themselves prior art. AFE-ORB-SLAM combines truncated adaptive gamma, unsharp masking and an adaptive FAST threshold, gaining 34.65% average accuracy on OIVIO for 3.38% extra time ([Yu et al., JIRS 2022](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/80737/7/Yu_etal_JIRS_2022_AFE_ORB_SLAM_robust_monocular_VSLAM_based_on_adaptive_FAST_threshold_and_image_enhancement.pdf)). IRAF-SLAM triggers enhancement on local intensity statistics and adapts FAST thresholds from entropy, intensity and gradient. It was best or second-best on 7 of 8 EuRoC sequences, monocular only, with no runtime numbers ([arXiv:2507.07752](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07752)). Han et al. report more than 50% APE improvement for ORB-SLAM3 on ETH3D low light, but only the abstract was available ([IPIN 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/IPIN62893.2024.10786104)). Every prior EuRoC "darkening" was noise-free gamma (AirSLAM) or gamma plus "slight random noise" (DeepLine-VIO, [unverified snippet](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/25/16/5029)). None is physically correct.

**Real-time options.** All brightness arms fit trivially. Gamma, linear-gain and equalization LUTs are sub-millisecond on a CPU and stream through an FPGA. Learned curve estimators are tiny: Zero-DCE++ has 10K parameters and 0.115 GFLOPs at 1200x900x3 ([arXiv:2103.00860](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00860)), and SCI runs in 1.7 ms at 0.06 GFLOPs ([arXiv:2204.10137](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10137)). One inconsistency should be noted: the Zero-DCE abstract area gives 500 FPS at 640x480x3, while the Zero-DCE++ paper gives 500 FPS for Zero-DCE at 1200x900x3. Denoising is the binding constraint. FFDNet's 153 ms per frame on a 1050Ti, times two cameras, does not fit without a modern GPU or TensorRT. BM3D is offline only. No Jetson or FPGA runtime was found for Zero-DCE, SCI, BM3D or FFDNet at VGA resolution. The Jetson numbers that do exist are for VEViD (24.5 ms at 480p on a Nano, [Wikipedia summary, unverified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhyCV)) and a vendor blog ([unverified](https://www.edge-ai-vision.com/2026/09/full-stack-optimization-for-low-light-video-on-jetson-orin-nx-from-400-ms-to-28-ms/)).

**Baseline to beat.** Raw ORB-SLAM3 is the wrong baseline. The right one is **ORB-SLAM3 with its FAST thresholds scaled by the known attenuation**: `ORBextractor.iniThFAST` and `minThFAST` in the YAML, driven in deployment by the analog-gain register and frame mean. MFGAN already lowered ORB-SLAM's FAST thresholds for its own night baseline ([Jung et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06632)). A brightening module that cannot beat that one-line change has no reason to exist.

**Documented harm.** This module has the richest record of corrections making SLAM worse. On OIVIO, HE-SLAM and IAGC-SLAM had **worse average accuracy than unmodified ORB-SLAM3** because "the noises contained in the images are also enhanced, and the over-enhancement exists in some regions"; CLAHE roughly tied ([AFE-ORB-SLAM](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/80737/7/Yu_etal_JIRS_2022_AFE_ORB_SLAM_robust_monocular_VSLAM_based_on_adaptive_FAST_threshold_and_image_enhancement.pdf)). On RobotCar night, stereo ORB-SLAM's mean relative error went from 16.94% on raw input to **25.06% with AHE, 18.80% with LIME and 35.64% with Deep Photo Enhancer**. The same classical enhancers helped stereo DSO ([MFGAN](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06632)). Global and adaptive histogram equalization made monocular ORB-SLAM **fail to initialize** on Gomez-Ojeda et al.'s 1-light sequence ([ICRA 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.01274)). Equalization produced matches that were "unreliable for localization purposes due to the presence of checkerboard artifacts" ([DarkSLAM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02199)). The Dual enhancer raised ORB-SLAM3 error on semi-dark sfm_house from 0.43 to 0.54 m and on KITTI 07 from 2.49 to 4.85 m ([Twilight SLAM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11310)).

### Motion blur: stereo-inertial ORB-SLAM3 already drops blurred frames and bridges them with the IMU

**Realistic conditions.** Rotation blur is about f·ω·t_e. With EuRoC's fx ≈ 458.65 px (verified in [ORB-SLAM3's EuRoC.yaml](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/UZ-SLAMLab/ORB_SLAM3/master/Examples/Stereo-Inertial/EuRoC.yaml)), 2 rad/s and a 10 ms exposure, that is about **9 px**. Translation at 2 m/s past a surface 2 m away adds about **4.6 px**, which is why the fast V1/V2 segments need depth-aware synthesis (both figures derived). **EuRoC's exposure time is not published anywhere examined.** Only relative exposures can be recovered photometrically ([Bergmann et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.02081)). TUM VI is the dataset to use when true exposure matters, since it records "accurate per-frame exposure times" ([arXiv:1804.06120](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.06120)). The Machine Hall sequences open with fast IMU-excitation motion whose blur is "challenging for visual tracking" ([EuRoC page](https://projects.asl.ethz.ch/datasets/euroc-mav/)). Real blur on this rig is therefore concentrated in initialization and in V1_03, V2_03 and MH_04/05, not spread through MH_01.

**Prior work.** The best quantitative evidence says blur makes ORB-SLAM drop frames, not inflate ATE. On blurred synthetic ArchViz, monocular ORB-SLAM went from 0.0138 to 0.1008 m ATE with 19.5% of frames dropped. On MBA-VO's own real dataset, a 752x480 global-shutter camera at 27 fps, ORB-SLAM dropped up to 36.8% of frames and failed on 2 of 5 sequences. The authors note that "the ATE metric is only computed from the successfully tracked frames" ([MBA-VO, ICCV 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.13684)). **MBA-VO contains no EuRoC experiments**; see the verification section. The decisive EuRoC result comes from Gjerde et al. MAXIM deblurring plus saliency improved DSO's 11-sequence average from 0.335 to 0.260 m, while **DM-VIO with no deblurring averaged 0.066 m**. The authors conclude the IMU "improves the overall result for other factors as well" ([Gjerde et al., ICMVA 2024](https://vbn.aau.dk/ws/files/770410304/3653946.3653970.pdf)). IMU-derived kernels are never accurate enough to use raw. Joshi et al. found sensor noise "introduces significant errors" and needed a 5-minute drift search per image ([SIGGRAPH 2010](https://szeliski.org/papers/Joshi_ImageDeblurringIMUs_SG10.pdf)). Phones show 10–120 ms of variable camera-IMU delay ([Hu et al., CVPR 2016](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_cvpr_2016/papers/Hu_Image_Deblurring_Using_CVPR_2016_paper.pdf)). EuRoC's global-shutter, hardware-synchronized rig removes most of those phone-specific errors. Exposure length and translational blur remain unknown.

**Real-time options.** A gyro-kernel Wiener filter costs about 17 ms per 1080p frame on a GTX 1080. On real blur it doubled keypoint repeatability, from 26.8% to 52.8% ([FastGyro](https://arxiv.org/html/1805.08542v1)). DeblurGAN-v2-MobileNet costs 38.1 ms per 752x480 frame on a laptop RTX 2080 ([MBA-VO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.13684)). Multi-frame video deblurrers cost 0.15–0.21 s ([RT-Focuser comparison](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.21975)), and dense blur-aware SLAM runs at 0.36–2 fps. The feature-based analogue of MBA-VO's approach is to predict each keypoint's blur streak from the IMU and stereo depth, then inflate its reprojection covariance along the streak. That keeps the estimator consistent without synthesizing pixels. It is an inference, and no ORB-SLAM3 implementation was found. Frames 50 ms apart are not near-duplicates, so "lucky-frame" patch borrowing is weak at 20 Hz. It also double-counts information: restoring frame k from frame k−1 through a motion estimate makes the k−1 to k visual factor partly circular. That too is an inference.

**Baseline to beat.** Unmodified stereo-inertial ORB-SLAM3, which discards severely blurred frames and propagates with the IMU. No controlled ablation of "blurred versus skipped-with-IMU-bridging versus deblurred" inside one VIO was found, so the module must win on runs of consecutive blurred frames, keyframe density in fast segments and reset count.

**Documented harm.** Deblurring plus saliency was **worse than plain DSO on EuRoC MH1 (0.065 to 0.071 m) and V1_03 (0.538 to 0.629 m)** ([Gjerde et al.](https://vbn.aau.dk/ws/files/770410304/3653946.3653970.pdf)). Per-frame DeblurGAN-v2 raised ORB-SLAM's frame drops on ArchViz-2 from 1.068% to 2.763%, while lowering ATE on tracked frames ([MBA-VO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.13684)). The gyro-Wiener filter scored 18.55 dB against 23.41 dB for the blurred input at 10 px blur. DeblurGAN scored below the blurred input at every blur size ([DeepGyro](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.00986)). Ringing at small blur lengths is the reason any deblurring has to be gated on predicted blur length.

### Calibration drift: ORB-SLAM3 estimates none of it, yet tolerates most realistic extrinsic drift

**Realistic conditions.** Measured drift data is scarce. **No peer-reviewed measurement of camera-IMU extrinsic drift on a rigid rig was found**; every published camera-IMU number is an injected value. Stereo rigs give the anchors. Vibration on a 0.7 m UAV baseline caused "a few millimeters in translation and only tenth of a degree in rotation" ([Hinzmann et al., quoting Warren et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.06837)). Seven years and 11 recalibrations of a USV rig showed up to 2.5° and 8 mm, an upper envelope that includes handling ([Muhovič & Perš, Sensors 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7313687/)). A 20 °C warm-up moved image points by 0.3 px horizontally and 1.1 px vertically ([Adamczyk et al., Sensors 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6263942/)). EuRoC's camera and IMU are "strictly synchronized", so the true time offset is about zero and any offset has to be injected ([Qin & Shen, IROS 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.00692)). The research notes combine these anchors into injection tiers. The tiers are a synthesis, not a published standard:

| Parameter | Measured-realistic | Long-term or mishandled | Stress (for comparability) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stereo relative rotation | 0.02–0.2° | 0.5–2.5° | up to about 2° per axis |
| Stereo baseline | 0.1–1 mm | 2–8 mm | — |
| Principal point | 0.3–1 px | 2–10 px | ±10–50 px |

**Sensitivity on this rig (derived).** With fx ≈ 458.65 px and a 0.1101 m baseline ([EuRoC.yaml](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/UZ-SLAMLab/ORB_SLAM3/master/Examples/Stereo-Inertial/EuRoC.yaml)), a 0.1° relative yaw error is a 0.80 px disparity bias. That becomes a depth error of about 1.6% at 1 m, 7.9% at 5 m and 10.5% at 6.6 m, which is ORB-SLAM3's close/far threshold of 60 baselines. At 0.5° the errors are 8%, 40% and 52%. A 1 px principal-point offset equals about 0.125° of yaw, in line with Dang et al.'s first-order equivalence of image-centre offsets to yaw and pitch ([TIP 2009](https://www.mrt.kit.edu/z/publ/download/dangStillerHoffmannTIP09.pdf)). Pitch error shifts epipolar rows. Above about 0.25° it should start removing level-0 stereo matches, but that rests on a ±2 px row band recalled from ORB-SLAM's `ComputeStereoMatches` and should be checked in the fork. A 5 ms time offset at 1 rad/s mis-assigns about 2.3 px, more than 0.1–0.2° of extrinsic rotation. That fits VINS-Mono's measured ±6 ms tolerance on MH03 ([Qin & Shen](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.00692)).

**Prior work.** OpenVINS estimates intrinsics, extrinsics and time offset online. VINS-Mono/Fusion estimate extrinsics and time offset. ROVIO estimates extrinsics. OKVIS uses a fixed image delay ([OpenVINS](https://udel.edu/~pgeneva/downloads/papers/c10.pdf); [OKVIS config](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ethz-asl/okvis/master/config/config_fpga_p2_euroc.yaml)). **ORB-SLAM3 estimates no calibration online.** `Stereo.T_c1_c2`, `IMU.T_b_c1` and the intrinsics come from YAML, and there is no time-offset parameter ([Settings.cc](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/UZ-SLAMLab/ORB_SLAM3/master/src/Settings.cc)). Despite that, Zhu et al. found ORB-SLAM3 "the most balanced" under miscalibration on CERBERUS. Its robust costs suppress extrinsic error, but it shows "a strict linear dependency on the intrinsic matrix K" ([arXiv:2609.18628](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.18628)). Whether that run was stereo-inertial is not stated. Time offset is the parameter that bites. OKVIS without temporal calibration went from 0.284 m to 2.805 m (diverged) on MH03 as the offset grew from 5 to 30 ms. Online estimation recovered offsets to within about 0.3 ms in "a few seconds" ([Qin & Shen](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.00692)). Observability limits what any online estimator can do. EuRoC is an "under-actuated MAV with weakly excited acceleration and approximately 1-axis rotation". Under one-axis rotation, translation along that axis is unobservable, and under constant velocity the time offset is too ([Yang et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.09170)).

**Real-time options.** Detection is cheap, and the inputs already exist. For pitch and roll drift, track the median and MAD of the left-right row offset over stereo matches. Yaw drift is invisible in row offsets and needs a temporal check, such as disparity against IMU-predicted depth change. A visual-versus-preintegrated rotation residual tracks camera-IMU drift. Correlating gyro rate with optical-flow rotation tracks time offset. These are inferences built from the data ORB-SLAM3 already has. In simulation, Xiao et al. detected injected errors of 0.09° and 0.9 cm, and the metrics "immediately return to normal" once the displacement is removed ([RA-L 2022](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~xiao/papers/extrinsics.pdf)). Estimation costs more. Adding calibration states to a filter cost +3.6 ms per image ([Yang et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.09170)). Stereo rotation tracking reached 0.02–0.06° repeatability at 45 ms per frame, 20 ms of it feature detection that ORB-SLAM3 already performs ([Ling & Shen](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.10705)). Any runtime stereo update has to rebuild the rectification maps that ORB-SLAM3 computes at startup from `T_c1_c2`; this was read from `Settings`, so confirm it in the fork. Updates should be gated on an excitation test and frozen during hover or straight cruise.

**Baseline to beat.** Unmodified ORB-SLAM3 at each injected tier, with an external OpenVINS run with online calibration as the reference estimator. In OpenVINS simulation, a bad initial calibration diverged to 508.7 m with online calibration off and recovered to 0.139 m with it on, against about 0.13 m with true calibration ([OpenVINS](https://udel.edu/~pgeneva/downloads/papers/c10.pdf)). The module must also report a detection ROC, since none exists on EuRoC.

**Documented harm.** On EuRoC Vicon-room sequences, adding online IMU-intrinsic calibration gave **1.46–1.52° / 0.094–0.103 m against 1.403° / 0.090 m without it**. The authors attribute this to EuRoC's near-degenerate motion ([Yang et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.09170)). Residual-based monitors can also miss the errors that matter. On KITTI, calibration reprojection error stayed almost flat with respect to fx and cu (less than 0.001 px change) while odometry accuracy changed substantially ([Cvišić et al., ECMR 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.03462)).

### Post-SLAM trajectory correction: only run-specific jitter and glitches are reachable without extra information

**Realistic conditions.** The published stereo-inertial MH01 ATE is **0.036 m, median of 10 runs, SE(3) alignment** ([ORB-SLAM3, T-RO 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.11898)). The lab measures about 39 mm with a run-to-run SD of about 4 mm. The lab's per-edge relative-pose error is heavy-tailed (excess kurtosis +29 to +54), slightly anti-correlated (lag-1 autocorrelation −0.04 to −0.2), anisotropic, and dominated by an error profile shared by every run. The autocorrelation reveals structure. Pure per-pose jitter (independent absolute error on each pose) gives edges a lag-1 autocorrelation of exactly −0.5. Mixing it with independent edge noise gives ρ = −σ_j²/(2σ_j² + σ_e²), so the jitter share of edge variance is −2ρ, about **8–40%**. A positively autocorrelated shared profile would mask some of the jitter, which makes that range a rough lower bound. This is derived arithmetic. A banded edge covariance or explicit per-pose jitter terms describe the data better than i.i.d. edge noise.

**What cannot be corrected.** Visual-inertial navigation has four unobservable directions, global position and yaw ([Hesch et al., T-RO 2014](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~stergios/papers/TRO_2014_OC_VINS_Joel_Dimitris_Sean.pdf)). Smooth drift and slow scale error are smooth by construction, so a smoothness prior cannot tell them apart from motion. The shared profile is a deterministic function of scene and motion. No method that sees one run plus a generic prior can identify it, and pooling across runs removes only the run-specific part. The post-SLAM methods with real gains all **learn a systematic, input-conditioned bias from ground truth on other sequences**. DPC-Net cut KITTI-00 translational ATE from 60.22 to 15.68 m, but against a weak sparse stereo VO baseline ([ar5iv 1709.03128](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1709.03128)). A GP bias correction gave about 10% for less than 1% runtime ([Tang et al., CRV 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.04678)). De Maio and Lacroix learned bias and heteroscedastic covariance jointly ([arXiv:2007.14943](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.14943)). Sparse absolute anchors also work. Pre-surveyed markers in a pose graph cut revisit inconsistency by 97.9–99.1% in under 0.25 s ([Giberna et al., arXiv:2608.16281](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16281)). **No published paper post-processes ORB-SLAM3 or any VI-SLAM EuRoC trajectory and reports a lower ATE.**

**The lab's denoiser in this light.** The bilevel SE(3) denoiser has an inner pose-graph solve differentiated through the implicit function theorem, and an outer objective of measurement fidelity plus a smoothness prior plus sparse anchors. Its closest published relatives are IFT-based covariance learning, which reduced synthetic SE(2) test RMSE from 2.597 to 0.775 ([Qadri et al., ICRA 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.09718)), and closed-form joint state-and-covariance estimation ([Khosoussi & Shames, arXiv:2502.04584](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04584)). A reviewer will expect both as baselines. Its smoothness term is close to a white-noise-on-acceleration GP prior. Its anchors are **exact ground-truth poses at 2 of every 100 frames**. That makes it a ground-truth-at-test-time method, and every result must disclose it. Two consequences follow for planning. First, the denoiser must be compared against an **anchors-only correction**: the same anchors in a plain pose graph, or piecewise rubber-sheeting between them, with no learned weights and no smoothness prior. Without that comparison, the gain from denoising cannot be separated from the gain from 2% ground truth. Second, a deployable version replaces exact ground truth with realistic marker-survey noise and sweeps anchor density, which recasts it as marker-constrained correction in the sense of Giberna et al. It has so far been validated only on i.i.d. Gaussian edge noise. Synthetic tests should add a shared deterministic profile, Student-t or contaminated noise, and per-pose jitter. **No results from the exp42/exp43 line are used anywhere in this report.** An audit found that line optimizes directly against ground truth at every pose; the audit is reported separately. Cross-run consensus remains a legitimate design idea: an edge that disagrees with other runs at the same timestamp is a glitch. But its performance is unestablished.

**Real-time options and baseline.** Robust kernels run at real-time cost. An adaptive Barron-type kernel beat fixed Huber on KITTI ICP (1.18°/2.03% against 1.32°/2.27%), and both fixed kernels failed on sequence 04 ([Chebrolu et al., RA-L 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.14938)). DCS needs no extra variables ([Agarwal et al., ICRA 2013](https://www.ipb.uni-bonn.de/wp-content/papercite-data/pdf/agarwal13icra.pdf)). GNC tolerates 70–80% outliers without an initial guess ([Yang et al., RA-L 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.08605)). The deployable split is to learn weights, kernel shape and any bias model offline on held-out sequences, then run a fixed-lag robust smoother with them online. The baselines are raw ORB-SLAM3 (median of at least 10 runs), ORB-SLAM3 plus a final full BA, an L2 smoother, Huber or Cauchy smoothers, an adaptive kernel, DCS on odometry edges, Khosoussi and Shames, and the anchors-only correction above. **Documented harm:** none was found specifically for post-hoc ORB-SLAM3 EuRoC correction. The general risk is that ground-truth alignment absorbs drift, so report unaligned (first-pose-anchored) error next to aligned ATE.

### Post-SLAM map correction: densify and deform rather than denoise sparse points

ORB-SLAM3 outputs sparse keyframes and map points, so map "correction" in practice means densification plus keeping the dense map consistent after the trajectory changes. Probabilistic semi-dense depth fusion over ORB-SLAM keyframes yields "clean reconstructions with very few outliers" and argues for decoupling mapping from tracking ([Mur-Artal & Tardós, RSS 2015](https://roboticsproceedings.org/rss11/p41.html)). CodeMapping predicts uncertainty-aware dense depth per keyframe in a separate thread, is demonstrated with ORB-SLAM3, and fuses into a TSDF ([arXiv:2107.08994](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.08994)). Photo-SLAM seeds Gaussian primitives from ORB-SLAM3 and even improved pose accuracy on Replica RGB-D, **0.604 cm against ORB-SLAM3's 1.833 cm ATE**. It tracks at 18–20 FPS on a Jetson AGX Orin ([arXiv:2311.16728](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.16728v2)). Kimera-PGMO deforms the mesh through a deformation graph when the pose graph changes, so it does not need to rebuild ([GitHub](https://github.com/MIT-SPARK/Kimera-PGMO)). That is the mechanism that should consume any post-SLAM trajectory correction. For EuRoC stereo, the cheapest path is stereo matching on keyframes, TSDF fusion and marching cubes, followed by re-integration or deformation after correction. Map accuracy should be scored against the Vicon-room point-cloud ground truth distributed with EuRoC V1/V2; no published accuracy comparison for ORB-SLAM3-plus-TSDF on that data was found. Learned point-cloud denoising exists ([Luo & Hu, ICCV 2021](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2021/html/Luo_Score-Based_Point_Cloud_Denoising_ICCV_2021_paper.html)) but has never been evaluated on SLAM maps. **Documented harm:** learned map-point filtering on ORB-SLAM3 improved KITTI 04 ATE by 25.9%, and the authors themselves call the results "mixed performance across sequences" ([PCR-ORB, arXiv:2512.23318, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.23318)).

## Evaluate the integrated pipeline at a 5 mm resolution, failures first

The lab's nondeterminism floor sets the resolution of every claim. With a run-to-run SD of 4 mm, the minimum detectable ATE difference at 80% power and two-sided α = 0.05 is 2.80·σ·√(2/n): **9.1 mm at n = 3, 7.1 mm at n = 5, 5.0 mm at n = 10 and 3.5 mm at n = 20** (derived). That is 23%, 18%, 13% and 9% of the 39 mm baseline. The fixed-pattern stress result (+9.5 mm at n = 3, p = 0.07) sits exactly at the n = 3 detection limit, which explains its borderline p-value. Any module claiming less than about 5 mm needs 20 or more runs per arm. Post-SLAM smoothing will likely live in that regime. Run counts are therefore a first-class design parameter. ORB-SLAM3 was also the least repeatable system in a 20,000-run study, with hard crashes in more than 10% of runs across platforms ([Bujanca et al., arXiv:2109.13160](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.13160)). Log crashes separately from tracking failures, pin CPU affinity, and disable frequency scaling, as Bujanca et al. did.

Report validity before accuracy. Under synthetic corruption, ORB-SLAM2 returned a valid trajectory in only **37 of 63** image-space runs, and Gaussian noise, impulse noise and snow produced no evaluable trajectory at any severity ([Thomas et al., arXiv:2608.30690](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30690)). The reporting order should be fixed. First, the valid-run rate. Second, tracking coverage, as the fraction of ground-truth timestamps with a tracked pose or as Bujanca's correct-rate-of-tracking. Third, Atlas map count and resets. Only then ATE RMSE under SE(3) alignment, which is ORB-SLAM3's convention for every non-monocular mode ([ORB-SLAM3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.11898)), computed both on tracked frames and on all ground-truth timestamps including IMU-propagated poses. MBA-VO shows why both are needed: ORB-SLAM's tracked-frame ATE looked good precisely because it dropped the hard frames. Add RPE at several lengths, per-axis error, per-module latency, end-to-end latency against throughput, and power on Kria or Jetson.

Run the ablation in stages; do not start with a full factorial. Stage one tests each module alone on its own corruption, swept in physical units: ΔEV and gain for light; exposure in milliseconds along the ground-truth/IMU trajectory with depth-based warping for blur; degrees, millimetres, pixels and milliseconds for calibration. Use bisection for the failure threshold, following SLAM Adversarial Lab ([Hefny et al., arXiv:2603.17165](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17165)). Stage two tests the integrated system on compound corruption in three configurations: all on, all off, and leave-one-out. That is k + 2 configurations instead of 2^k, and leave-one-out exposes interactions. Each module is also tested gated versus always on. The full 2^4 factorial is reserved for the final shortlist. **Low light and blur are physically coupled**, because real auto-exposure lengthens exposure before it raises gain. The compound case should therefore trade exposure against gain at fixed photon budget, not stack independent corruptions. Dynamic perturbations are harder than static ones ([Xu et al., arXiv:2406.16850](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16850)), so include dimming ramps and lights-off steps that exercise each module's gating and hysteresis.

Two guards keep the integrated claims honest. First, synthetic proxies only sometimes preserve real-world rankings. On 4Seasons, a brightness-plus-contrast proxy favoured DROID-SLAM by 5.51 m while the real evening sequence favoured DPVO by 10.85 m ([Thomas et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30690)). Every tuned module must therefore be frozen and re-tested on real adverse data, and any disagreement between synthetic and real rankings must be reported. Second, the ground-truth leakage rules from trajectory evaluation apply to every module that learns or tunes. Train and tune on sequences disjoint from the test sequence, ideally leave-one-sequence-out across MH_01–05 and V*. Never estimate weights or kernel parameters from ground-truth residuals on the test sequence. Align only once, at evaluation. Label any use of ground-truth anchors at test time.

## A low-light experiment that separates contrast from noise

The experiment answers one question: once physically correct darkening is applied, does any correction beat simply lowering the FAST threshold, and if so, at what severity does denoising start to matter? It proceeds in six phases, ordered so that the cheapest phases can kill the expensive ones.

**Phase 0: measure the starting point.** For all 11 EuRoC sequences, compute per-frame mean DN, the fraction of pixels below 20 DN, the number of distinct gray levels, and the left/right brightness ratio. This establishes how dark MH_04/05 and V*_03 actually are, as the notes recommend, since EuRoC carries no exposure metadata. Check linearity. Whether companding or a tone curve was active on the VI-sensor is unverified, and the pipeline assumes linear DN. Validate the assumed noise model by running a single-image noise-level estimator ([Liu, Tanaka & Okutomi](https://www.mathworks.com/matlabcentral/fileexchange/36921-noise-level-estimation-from-a-single-image)) on clean EuRoC frames and on synthetic frames, and compare the recovered signal-dependent variance. If the lab has an MT9V034 module, a short EMVA 1288 photon-transfer measurement ([EMVA 1288 R3.1](https://www.emva.org/wp-content/uploads/EMVA1288-3.1a.pdf)) would replace the parameter bracket below with measured values. **No published MT9V034 full-well, conversion-gain or read-noise figure exists.**

**Phase 0 also fixes the simulation, which works in physical units.** Darkening follows the EMVA/ELD linear camera model ([ELD, CVPR 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.12751)). Convert DN to photoelectrons, scale by 2^−ΔEV, sample Poisson shot noise, add read noise in electrons, apply analog gain g = min(2^ΔEV, 4), quantize to 10 bits, and only then derive the 8-bit output and apply corrections. There is no demosaic, white-balance or tone-curve inversion, because EuRoC is raw monochrome, so the heavy part of Brooks-style unprocessing drops out ([Brooks et al., CVPR 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11127)). In the gain-compensated levels the output brightness is unchanged, so the frame's own noise (about 1.4 DN) is not shrunk. Add only the variance difference between the target and the inherited noise, per pixel, in the manner of Noctif3R's reference-noise subtraction ([arXiv:2609.21114](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21114)). Draw any fixed-pattern noise once per sequence and hold it fixed. Apply the same ΔEV to both cameras, since the scene dims for both. One extra arm adds a ±0.5 EV left/right offset to mimic EuRoC's independent auto-exposure and to test shared against independent stereo correction. Release seeds and parameters.

The severity ladder is bounded by the sensor's 4x analog gain. The table carries the experiment's predictions. **L1–L2 are the realistic gain-compensated regime.** Noise rises only to 2–3 DN and every threshold is unchanged, so no arm should differ from clean beyond the MDE. That extends the lab's null results for fixed-pattern and IMU noise. **L3–L5 are the contrast-collapse regime.** Unmodified ORB-SLAM3 needs 2–8x more clean contrast to detect a corner, and fallback cells take over. Lowering the threshold and brightening should recover most of the loss, because restored noise (5–16 DN) is still below 20. At L5 the restored noise reaches 13–25 DN against a threshold of 20. **L5–L6 is therefore where brightness-plus-denoising should first separate from brightness alone.** The crossover moves by about ±0.5 EV across the read-noise bracket. At L6–L7 the primary threshold can no longer be met by any contrast, 8-bit posterization reaches 16–32 DN steps, and only the 10-bit path or denoising can recover anything. The integer threshold control floors at 1 and saturates. FAST's 9-pixel contiguous-arc test makes noise-triggered corners rarer than a per-pixel comparison suggests, so the crossover estimate is a prediction to test, not a result.

**Phase 1 is detector-level and needs no SLAM runs.** For every arm, level and sequence, with three noise seeds, measure FAST keypoints per grid cell, the fraction of cells forced to the fallback threshold, stereo matches, and descriptor match rate. Because synthetic darkening is pixel-aligned with the clean frame, **keypoint repeatability and descriptor agreement against the clean frame can be computed exactly**, with no geometry. This phase takes minutes to hours. It will show where C1 and C2 diverge and where noise corners appear, and it prunes the SLAM grid.

**Phase 2 is the core SLAM grid on MH_01.** Run arms C0–C5 at L1–L7 with **10 paired noise seeds**. Seed s generates one darkened sequence, and every arm processes that same sequence, so arm comparisons are paired on noise realization while ORB-SLAM3's thread nondeterminism stays independent. Add 20 clean L0 runs to re-establish the floor. That is 440 runs. At the two levels where Phase 1 shows the largest C2–C4 gap, increase to 20 seeds, bringing the MDE to 3.5 mm. **Phase 3 tests dynamic darkness on MH_01**: a 30 s dimming ramp from L0 to L5, and a 2 s lights-off step to L6. The step is modelled on UMA-VI's sudden darkness. Arms are C0, C1-d, gated C2 and gated C4, with 10 seeds each (80 runs). This phase tests trigger latency and whether gating flicker hurts map-point re-observation, which no paper has measured. **Phase 4 tests generalization**: MH_02–05, V1_01–03 and V2_01–03 at L3 and L5, with arms C0, C1, the best brightness arm and the best brightness-plus-denoise arm, 5 seeds each (400 runs). V2_01 at matched levels also allows a direct comparison with AirSLAM's gamma-only Dark EuRoC. **Phase 5 tests sensitivity to the sensor model**: L4 and L5 at the four corners of the read-noise (27/58 e⁻) and full-well (15,000/33,000 e⁻) bracket, with C1, C2 and C4 at 5 seeds (120 runs). This shows that the conclusions do not hinge on the unpublished sensor constants. The EuRoC total is about 1,040 SLAM runs. Each runs at about real time, so the campaign is tens of CPU-hours and parallelizes across cores, provided affinity is pinned.

Statistics and decision rules should be fixed before running. The primary endpoint per level and arm combines two outcomes. A failed or invalid run is ranked worse than any valid run, and the arms are compared with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test paired on seed. A Fisher exact test on valid-run counts and the median ATE of valid runs are reported alongside. The **pre-registered primary contrast is C4 against C2 at L5**. The secondary contrasts are C2 against C1 across all levels and C8 against C2 at L5–L6. Correct across levels with Holm. The C1–C2 equivalence should be tested with a two one-sided test at a ±5 mm margin, because "brightening equals a lowered threshold" is a claim of equivalence, not merely a failure to find a difference. The decision rule follows from the predictions. If C4 beats C2 by more than the MDE, or lifts the valid-run rate, at some level that is physically reachable, build the denoiser and trigger it on gain x restoration factor. If C2 beats C1, the descriptor-tie mechanism is real, and a brightness LUT earns its place in the PL. If C1 ≈ C2 ≈ C4 everywhere, the low-light module reduces to a gain-aware adaptive FAST threshold, which is a config change plus a register read. That is a publishable result, and the design is built to detect it. If C8 beats C2, the correction belongs before 8-bit truncation on the Kria. Any arm must also stay within the MDE of clean at L0, so gating has to be an identity on clean input.

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing of the low-light module from "pick the best enhancer" to "prove you beat a threshold". For a feature pipeline with fixed absolute thresholds, darkness on this sensor is first a contrast problem and only later a noise problem. The 1x–4x gain window means the realistic regime (up to 2 EV) is predicted to be harmless. The interesting regime begins where the camera runs out of gain, and denoising enters only about two to three stops later. The experiment is built so its most likely outcome is cheap and still informative. A gain-aware threshold plus a 10-bit LUT in the Kria's logic may capture most of the benefit, and a denoiser earns its place only past about 4–5 EV. Either result would be the first such measurement on a stereo-inertial system.

The same logic applies to the other modules. Blur on this rig is already absorbed by frame dropping and IMU bridging. Calibration drift at measured magnitudes mostly falls under ORB-SLAM3's robust costs, apart from intrinsics and time offset. Post-SLAM smoothing can reach only the run-specific minority of an error that is mostly shared across runs. In each case the credible contribution is a controlled measurement against the strongest trivial baseline, at a run count matched to the 4 mm floor and with ground-truth use disclosed, not a new restoration network. The largest untested lever sits upstream of every module: on the live Kria rig, exposure/gain policy and pre-truncation correction in programmable logic can do things no correction applied to recorded EuRoC data can.
